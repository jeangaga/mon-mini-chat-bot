# A. JPY WEEK — MACRO SYNTHESIS

Japan’s macro configuration shifted toward **stronger corporate resilience alongside an acute imported/upstream inflation constraint**, while the BoJ reaction function hardened materially toward a prepared September hike. August Reuters Tankan partially reversed July’s services weakness while semiconductor-led manufacturing confidence strengthened further, reducing the growth-fragility argument against normalization. June’s balance-of-payments data meanwhile confirmed that expensive energy, freight and rapid import growth are materially worsening Japan’s near-term terms of trade even though the structural external position remains strong and the headline current-account deficit was heavily distorted by dividend timing. Money data remain consistent with accommodative but non-accelerating financial conditions rather than domestic monetary overheating.

Inflation remains the decisive policy constraint. July CGPI stayed exceptionally high at 7.2% y/y and yen-basis import prices were close to 30%, but the monthly flow slowed to +0.1% and to 0.0% excluding the seasonal electricity surcharge: **high inflation stock / softer marginal flow**. The central-bank tape materially strengthens the policy interpretation because the 10 August Summary of Opinions revealed that **at least three of nine members** argued for a faster pace of tightening than the established roughly twice-yearly cadence, while “many” placed greater weight on overshoot risk. The July 8–1 hold therefore understated the hawkish distribution. Joint U.S.–Japan intervention was also officially confirmed and the September communication runway was assembled. The regime is consequently **resilient activity + severe imported inflation + increasingly pre-emptive BoJ**, but the final validation still depends on CPI/pass-through: the Bank is preparing to act ahead of full consumer-price confirmation, even as the marginal producer-price impulse begins to cool.

2. Professional Market Commentary

The headline was a very large current-account miss: Japan moved into a ¥92.3bn deficit in June, its first monthly deficit in 17 months, versus a Reuters consensus for a ¥1.512tn surplus.

The dominant professional interpretation, however, was composition over headline rather than a sudden structural external-balance break. Reuters identified a 74% collapse in the primary-income surplus to roughly ¥380bn, driven largely by larger dividend payments to overseas investors, as the main reason for the swing.

The adverse trade / terms-of-trade channel nevertheless strengthened materially underneath the dividend distortion. Official detail showed imports rising much faster than exports, a deterioration in the goods balance, sharply higher oil-import costs and a substantial increase in sea-freight payments. Seasonally adjusted imports rose 6.1% m/m while the SA goods balance swung to a ¥580.8bn deficit.

That weakness sits against a very different medium-term external picture. Japan's current-account surplus for H1 2026 reached a record ¥17.4tn, +22.5% y/y, supported by stronger trade performance and semiconductor-related exports.

No qualifying independent named-economist debate was identified.

3. Comment — Economist Layer

This is a real deterioration in the monthly external-account configuration, but the ¥92.3bn headline deficit materially overstates the structural deterioration.

Signal hierarchy:

* headline: first deficit in 17 months and a ¥1.604tn downside surprise
* seasonally adjusted current account: ¥1.397tn versus ¥3.065tn previously
* trade leg: SA balance moved from +¥204.5bn to -¥580.8bn
* import momentum: SA imports +6.1% m/m versus exports -1.6%
* energy shock: crude-import value surged despite falling physical volumes, pointing to a price / FX rather than domestic-demand impulse
* shipping-cost channel: the wider transport and sea-freight deficits confirm that the external supply shock is feeding directly into service imports
* primary income: the exceptional June compression was dominated by dividend payments to non-resident investors rather than a collapse in Japan's overseas earning capacity
* structural cross-check: the H1 current-account surplus remained a record ¥17.43tn, strongly arguing against a conventional balance-of-payments-stress interpretation.

The key distinction is between two separate negatives.

First, the primary-income collapse is largely a timing / composition effect. Reuters attributes much of the decline to larger dividend payouts, while the half-year data show that Japan's underlying overseas-income machine remains extremely large.

Second, the deterioration in the trade and services legs is more macro-relevant. The prior Japan sequence had already identified expensive energy and yen weakness as compressing the external surplus while investment income buffered the current account. June confirms that mechanism: imports accelerate sharply and the seasonally adjusted trade balance turns negative.

The useful signal is therefore not “Japan has lost its structural external surplus.” It is that the lagged energy / import-cost deterioration has arrived, while semiconductor exports and foreign-asset income continue to prevent that deterioration from becoming an external-funding problem.

The large monthly financial-account movements and errors and omissions should receive lower cyclical weight. They make the financing arithmetic noisy and should not be mapped mechanically into a durable JPY funding signal without confirmation.

State transition: deterioration — the import-cost shock is now visible in the trade and services accounts, while the headline current-account deficit is amplified by dividend timing rather than signalling structural external stress.

Context from prior regional live file:

Japan's prior trade sequence already showed an export-resilient but energy-squeezed economy: June customs trade recorded exports +19.3% y/y but imports +25.4%, with the import-cost shock identified as the principal marginal deterioration. The June BoP confirms that configuration at the broader external-account level. Japan's structural external position remains strong, but the near-term terms-of-trade burden has clearly worsened.

4. HF Take — Strategist Layer

The clean market interpretation is JPY-negative at the external-flow margin, but not a structural bearish balance-of-payments signal.

For JPY:

* the ¥1.6tn headline miss and worsening SA trade balance reduce near-term fundamental external support
* higher oil and freight costs strengthen the adverse weak-yen / import-price feedback loop
* the record H1 surplus and continued foreign-investment-income strength argue against extrapolating June into a persistent deficit regime
* the dividend distortion makes the NSA headline substantially less informative than the trade/import deterioration underneath it.

For the BoJ reaction function, the signal is two-sided.

Higher imported energy and transport costs can sustain headline and corporate inflation and reinforce concern about FX-driven import-price pass-through. But this is poor-quality inflation from a policy perspective: the same shock compresses household real income and corporate margins rather than demonstrating stronger domestic demand.

The release therefore raises the inflation constraint without independently establishing that domestic activity can absorb materially faster normalization.

The key verification is whether the external-price shock remains a relative-price event or spreads into wages, services prices and inflation expectations.

2. Professional Market Commentary

3. Comment — Economist Layer

This is a meaningful improvement in Japan's corporate-confidence pulse, and importantly it repairs part of the weakness visible in July.

Signal hierarchy:

* services direction: +28 from +25 reverses part of July's seven-point deterioration
* services breadth: gains spread across wholesale, information services and other services rather than being concentrated in one category
* manufacturing momentum: +18 from +13 represents a further acceleration in the already-strong semiconductor / AI-linked industrial channel
* chemicals: +33 from +23
* metal and machinery: +25 from +12
* autos: transport equipment at zero remains the principal sectoral hole
* forward signal: manufacturing expectations at +16 imply some expected cooling, while services are expected to hold their August improvement
* survey quality: 219 of 510 firms responded, making the Reuters Tankan timely but narrower than the official BOJ Tankan.

The most important development is not simply the +3-point non-manufacturing headline.

July had produced an uncomfortable split: semiconductor-linked manufacturing remained resilient at +13 while services fell to +25 under pressure from imported costs, yen weakness and higher interest rates. August improves both legs simultaneously — manufacturing accelerates while services regain some ground.

The semiconductor evidence is also stronger than a single sentiment headline. Survey respondents described unusually strong order intake in chip-related machinery, fitting the broader AI-capex support already visible in the Japan industrial and export sequence.

The caveat is extrapolation. Manufacturing remains disproportionately driven by semiconductor-linked industries, autos have not joined the improvement and firms themselves expect some easing by November.

A remaining question is whether exceptional chip orders represent durable final AI demand or contain some precautionary / pull-forward component.

State transition: reversal — July's services deterioration partially unwinds while semiconductor-led manufacturing strength accelerates.

Context from prior regional live file:

July Reuters Tankan had shown non-manufacturing sentiment falling to +25 from +32 while semiconductor-supported manufacturing held at +13, leaving Japan's corporate picture resilient but sharply bifurcated. August partially reverses that services deterioration and strengthens the manufacturing leg. The corporate-growth signal therefore becomes more broadly constructive again, although it remains heavily dependent on technology demand.

4. HF Take — Strategist Layer

This is incrementally supportive of BOJ normalization, but it is not a standalone tightening trigger.

The useful reaction-function signal is cumulative:

* stronger services confidence reduces the argument that imported-cost pressure is already producing a broad domestic-demand break
* accelerating manufacturing confidence reinforces the evidence that global AI / semiconductor demand is providing Japan with an industrial buffer
* continued weakness in autos and softer November manufacturing expectations argue against treating the print as evidence of generalized overheating
* the Reuters survey remains secondary to wages, CPI, hard household activity and the official Tankan

This resilience matters more given the BOJ's July Summary of Opinions. Board-member submissions emphasized that AI demand was cushioning the adverse terms-of-trade shock and that Japan's economy had shown resilience despite the Middle East shock. Several opinions also argued that upside price risks deserved greater weight and that the pace of hikes might need to become faster or more nimble depending on the data.

JPY / JGB mapping: mildly supportive of the normalization / JPY side at the margin, mainly by removing some growth-fragility risk rather than adding a new inflation shock.

A stronger hawkish interpretation would require the confidence improvement to translate into wages, investment and domestic demand rather than remain predominantly semiconductor-driven.

Japan — M2 Money Supply / Broad Money (Jul)

Release Date: 12 Aug 2026 | Local Time: 01:50
Importance: **

1. Reuters Data

M2 Money Supply: 1 293 588 300M | Prior: 1 291 809 700M | Revised: 1 291 493 200M

Broad Money: 2 330,6T | Prior: 2 327,9T | Revised: 2 328,2T

2. Professional Market Commentary

No Tier 1 institutional debate detected.

The useful framing is record liquidity levels but no renewed acceleration in the monetary impulse.

The BOJ's official July release showed M2 growth unchanged at 2.2% y/y after the slowdown from May's 2.5%, while broadly-defined liquidity eased marginally to 4.4% y/y. Sequentially, however, M2 rebounded at a 2.0% seasonally adjusted annualized rate after June's revised -0.9%, so the prior month's outright contraction did not continue.

Composition was much stronger in financial assets than in transaction money. Currency in circulation fell 1.2% y/y, deposit money rose only 0.2% and quasi-money increased 4.3%. Within broadly-defined liquidity, investment trusts accelerated to +13.1% y/y and reached ¥126.0tn, while foreign bonds rose 6.1% and government securities 18.8%.

3. Comment — Economist Layer

This release reinforces the distinction between liquidity level and liquidity momentum.

Key hierarchy:

* M2 level: ¥1,293.6tn on the seasonally adjusted BOJ basis
* M2 annual growth: unchanged at 2.2%, below May's 2.5% peak
* monthly impulse: +2.0% annualized after June's revised -0.9%
* broad liquidity: record ¥2,338.4tn on the NSA measure, but y/y growth edged down to 4.4%
* transaction money: weak, with cash -1.2% and deposit money only +0.2%
* quasi-money: +4.3%, consistent with stronger demand for interest-bearing deposit-type instruments
* portfolio composition: investment trusts +13.1% and foreign bonds +6.1% show substantial financial-asset accumulation outside conventional deposits.

The July rebound matters because June had raised the question of whether BOJ normalization was beginning to generate a persistent contraction in private-sector liquidity.

July does not validate that concern: the monthly M2 decline reverses, annual M2 growth remains positive and broad liquidity stays firm.

But the opposite interpretation — renewed monetary acceleration — is also too strong. M2's y/y growth peaked at 2.5% in May and has remained at 2.2% for two months.

The money data therefore remain consistent with gradual transmission from normalization without monetary stress, rather than either restrictive contraction or an excess-liquidity reacceleration.

The investment-trust surge should be treated primarily as portfolio allocation rather than direct nominal-demand evidence. Likewise, the foreign-bond component mixes active accumulation with FX valuation effects and should not be mapped mechanically into a JPY-outflow signal.

State transition: continuation — normalization is slowing the monetary-growth impulse at the margin, but broad private-sector liquidity continues to expand and July reverses June's monthly contraction.

Context from prior regional live file:

Earlier money-stock releases had already shown that Japan's inflation shock was not being accompanied by an obvious domestic monetary acceleration. July preserves that configuration: money remains plentiful and financial conditions are not contracting sharply, but the growth impulse is far less striking than the inflation pipeline. The inflation story therefore continues to run primarily through costs, FX and pass-through rather than monetary overheating.

4. HF Take — Strategist Layer

Standalone JPY / JGB relevance remains low.

For the BOJ reaction function, the release is broadly neutral:

* it does not show monetary conditions becoming sufficiently restrictive to force policy caution
* it does not show a fresh domestic-liquidity boom that would strengthen the case for faster tightening
* continued positive M2 and broad-liquidity growth leave room for gradual normalization
* slowing y/y momentum argues against using monetary aggregates as evidence of domestic-demand overheating
* portfolio rotation into investment trusts is structurally interesting but should not be mapped directly into consumption or inflation

The BOJ's July Summary of Opinions similarly characterized financial conditions as accommodative, with negative short-term real rates, proactive lending attitudes and increasing demand for funds.

The more important Japan inflation assessment therefore remains wages, services prices, import costs and corporate pricing rather than accelerating money supply.

A materially more dovish monetary signal would require several months of outright M2 weakness accompanied by softer deposits and credit creation. July does not meet that threshold.

The institutional framing remained hawkish despite the downside surprise.

Japan's producer-price inflation held at an exceptionally elevated 7.2% y/y in July, only marginally below June's revised 7.3%. Reuters emphasized continued pressure from metals, AI-related demand, energy and yen weakness and linked the report to increased expectations of another BOJ hike.

The official BOJ detail gives the report an important second layer: near-term momentum cooled much more sharply than the annual rate suggests.

CGPI rose only 0.1% m/m after June's revised +0.5%, and the index excluding the seasonal summer-electricity surcharge was unchanged on the month.

Monthly composition was explicitly two-sided. Electricity / gas / water added +0.23pp, while petroleum and coal subtracted 0.20pp and chemicals 0.18pp as the earlier oil impulse retraced. Non-ferrous metals, food, plastics, electrical machinery and steel provided smaller positive contributions.

Masato Koike of Sompo Institute Plus nevertheless argued that wholesale inflation could reaccelerate if renewed Middle East tension lifted crude prices again, with further yen weakness providing an additional import-cost channel.

3. Comment — Economist Layer

This is a high-level inflation print with a materially softer marginal impulse. Both facts need to be retained.

Signal hierarchy:

* annual CGPI: 7.2% y/y — still close to June's revised 7.3%
* monthly CGPI: +0.1%, a 0.4pp deceleration from revised June and 0.5pp below consensus
* underlying July momentum: 0.0% m/m excluding summer electricity surcharges
* non-ferrous metals: +40.6% y/y
* chemicals: +12.9% y/y
* petroleum and coal: +17.5% y/y, but already subtracting from the July monthly move
* yen-basis import prices: +29.1% y/y
* contract-currency import prices: materially lower than the yen-basis rate, confirming that FX remains a major contributor to imported inflation
* AI supply chain: electronic components and memory-chip prices provide an additional non-energy cost channel.

The key distinction is stock versus flow.

The inflation stock remains uncomfortable. Producer prices are above 7%, yen-basis import prices are rising close to 30% and metals inflation is exceptionally strong. This validates the BOJ's concern that upstream price pressure could migrate into consumer prices.

But the July flow is clearly softer. The entire +0.1% monthly headline disappears once the seasonal electricity charge is removed, while petroleum and chemicals jointly subtract 0.38pp. July is therefore not another April-June-style acceleration month.

Some of the upstream inflation is reversible external cost pressure — oil, freight and the yen. Some is potentially stickier or structurally stronger — metals, AI-linked electronic components, food, utilities and domestic corporate price-setting.

JGM note:

July PPI remains extremely elevated but shows a first sign of peaking at the margin: 7.2% y/y after June's revised 7.3%.

The yen channel remains crucial. Import prices are around 29% y/y on a yen basis, with a roughly 11pp gap versus contract-currency inflation, showing the extent to which JPY depreciation amplifies the external cost shock.

JGM also highlights the acceleration in non-ferrous-metal inflation as an important AI-demand channel rather than a pure Middle East / oil story.

Three principal upside risks for the BOJ inflation outlook:

* Middle East / energy
* global AI-related demand and metals prices
* FX depreciation

The key concern is the PPI-to-CPI pass-through. The policy relevance of producer inflation depends increasingly on whether these upstream costs propagate into final consumer prices rather than simply compress corporate margins.

State transition: stabilization — upstream inflation remains extremely elevated and broad, but July breaks the prior sequence of rapid monthly acceleration.

Context from prior regional live file:

June CGPI had accelerated to a revised 7.3% y/y, after the prior sequence had already established energy, metals and weak-yen import prices as the main inflation shock. July leaves that high-level regime intact but sharply reduces monthly momentum, with prices flat once the seasonal electricity surcharge is removed. The inflation pipeline therefore remains dangerous, but it is no longer accelerating cleanly month after month.

4. HF Take — Strategist Layer

This remains hawkish for the BOJ reaction function, but the correct interpretation is more nuanced than the 7.2% y/y headline.

The BOJ has three reasons not to dismiss the release:

* wholesale inflation remains near its recent peak
* yen-based import inflation remains close to 30%
* pressure extends beyond energy into metals and AI-sensitive industrial inputs.

At the same time, the 0.1% m/m / 0.0% ex-electricity detail argues against treating July itself as a fresh inflation acceleration.

JGM policy overlay:

JGM reads the July BOJ Summary of Opinions as more hawkish than Ueda's post-meeting communication. That interpretation is directionally consistent with the official summary: several opinions argued for greater attention to upside inflation risks, a more nimble reaction function, and potentially a faster pace of hikes than market expectations.

The official Summary also states that underlying CPI inflation is approaching 2%, that inflation expectations have been rising and that Middle East developments, AI-related demand and yen depreciation all exert upward pressure on prices.

JGM therefore has a firmer expectation of a September hike.

Bessent's public encouragement of earlier BOJ tightening should not be interpreted as something the BOJ mechanically reacts to. JGM's point is instead that such communication can influence market expectations and therefore the pricing environment surrounding the BOJ. Reuters also identified Bessent's comments and recent FX-policy developments as part of the backdrop supporting September-hike expectations.

JGM working policy path:

* current policy rate: 1.00%
* September: +25bp
* January: +25bp

* working terminal-rate assumption: 1.75%

The tactical question is whether the BOJ shifts from roughly six-month intervals between hikes toward a three- to four-month cadence before eventually ending the normalization cycle.

JGM also flags September 2027 as a medium-term institutional consideration because future Board composition under Takaichi could become more dovish. This is a secondary horizon issue rather than a driver of the September 2026 decision.

Integrated policy read:

July CGPI by itself is softer at the margin, but this does not materially weaken the near-term hike case. The BOJ is confronting three separate upstream risks — Middle East energy prices, AI / metals demand and JPY weakness — while the July Summary of Opinions shows greater concern about upside inflation risk and the appropriate pace of normalization.

JPY / JGB mapping:

* front end: hawkish bias remains

# E. SIGNAL TENSION CHECK

Main contradiction:
The BoJ is becoming **more pre-emptively hawkish at the same moment that the marginal producer-price impulse is beginning to cool**. The Summary of Opinions reveals a broader faster-hike bloc and a shift toward overshoot prevention, yet July CGPI was flat excluding the seasonal electricity surcharge and much of the inflation shock still originates in energy, FX and imported costs rather than domestic overheating.

Why it matters:
September can be justified on a forward-looking pass-through/FX-risk framework even without an explosive CPI print, but the case for a faster subsequent cadence is much more dependent on whether the high producer/import-price stock actually propagates into consumer prices, wages and services.

Implication:
A September hike is the base case, but the **September-vs-terminal-path distinction is crucial**: soft pass-through may not prevent the first hike, yet it could quickly undermine the December/January follow-through and the broader faster-normalization thesis.

# H. CURRENCY MARKET-CONSENSUS SCAN

### Bottom line

**Pair convention: USD/JPY.**

The point-in-time institutional consensus entering the 9–15 August window was **medium-term constructive JPY but distinctly cautious tactically**. A Reuters FX poll conducted 31 July–5 August put the median USD/JPY forecast at **159 in three months, 157 in six months and 154 in twelve months**. That implied eventual yen appreciation, but only modest near-term improvement because intervention alone was not expected to overcome the still-large U.S.–Japan rate differential, weak Japanese growth fundamentals and persistent carry demand.

The publicly visible bank sample had the same basic shape but considerable dispersion. MUFG’s 3 August forecast was **158 for Q3 and 156 for Q4**, compared with Bloomberg consensus levels of 160 and 159 respectively. ING’s summer framework saw USD/JPY capable of remaining around 160 for several months, potentially approaching 165 before intervention, while maintaining a **158 year-end** forecast. BNP Paribas remained more cautious on JPY appreciation, forecasting **160 for Q4 2026**. The resulting public-bank Q4 cluster was therefore broad enough to reflect genuine disagreement over whether BOJ normalization could overcome carry and Japan’s energy/fiscal vulnerabilities.

Price action during the window was weaker for JPY than that medium-term forecast bias might suggest. By 14 August the yen was around **159.37 per dollar**, heading for its worst week in three months and again approaching the politically sensitive 160 area despite the earlier joint U.S.–Japan intervention. The underlying rate differential was still large enough to sustain carry demand. At the same time, BOJ expectations shifted materially: Reuters reported the market pricing a roughly **76% September-hike probability on 13 August**, versus 24% on 30 July, and nearly 80% by 14 August as reporting pointed toward an earlier and potentially faster hiking cycle.

**Public-bank Q4 2026 range:** **156–160 USD/JPY**

**Constructive JPY cluster:** **156–158 USD/JPY** — MUFG 156 Q4; ING 158 year-end.

**More cautious / tactical view:** **159–160**, with upside tail toward **165** while carry/rate differentials dominate — consistent with the Reuters near-term median, BNP’s Q4 forecast and ING’s tactical range.

**Bias at window close:** **constructive JPY medium term, but tactically fragile**. USD/JPY near 159.4 was already above MUFG’s Q3 target and close to the Reuters three-month median, while the yen had surrendered a significant part of its post-intervention gains despite sharply rising September BOJ-hike expectations.

### What changed through the data window

The window began after an unusually forceful late-July/early-August intervention episode had temporarily driven USD/JPY down sharply. The Reuters poll taken immediately afterward still concluded that intervention was **not a fundamental game changer**: strategists expected the yen to remain weak in the near term unless BOJ policy became more hawkish, rate differentials narrowed, energy prices declined or Japanese capital was repatriated.

During 12–14 August, two developments improved the medium-term JPY case without immediately producing sustained appreciation. Benign U.S. inflation data reduced Fed-hike expectations, while Japanese wholesale inflation and subsequent Reuters reporting materially increased expectations of a September BOJ hike and possibly a faster tightening cadence thereafter. Yet USD/JPY still hovered near 159.4–159.5 because the U.S.–Japan yield gap remained large and carry remained attractive.

The key evolution was therefore **policy-expectation convergence without equivalent spot convergence**. The probability of an early BOJ hike rose sharply, but the yen remained near the 160 intervention zone. That left the market effectively demanding either an actual BOJ delivery/faster path, a clearer turn in the Fed cycle or renewed intervention before abandoning carry-funded USD/JPY longs.

### Main consensus drivers

**BOJ normalization / rate differential:**
The dominant constructive-JPY thesis was that earlier and potentially more frequent BOJ hikes would narrow the still-large yield differential. By 14 August Reuters reported nearly 80% market pricing for a September hike, but the gap remained wide enough that expectations alone had not reversed carry demand.

**Intervention / policy coordination:**
The rare joint U.S.–Japan intervention changed the tactical distribution by placing a more credible cap around extreme USD/JPY upside, but the Reuters strategist poll explicitly judged intervention alone insufficient for sustained yen appreciation without a fundamental shift in rates or flows. Bank of America identified **160** as the key level where renewed coordinated intervention risk became material.

**Energy / terms of trade:**
Japan’s dependence on imported energy remained a negative JPY asymmetry. Higher energy prices worsen the trade balance and real-income backdrop even as they increase pressure on the BOJ to tighten, creating an unusually two-sided FX transmission channel.

**Fed / global dollar cycle:**
The medium-term JPY bull case also depended on the U.S. side. A durable reversal requires a turn in the dollar cycle as well as BOJ normalization. Softer U.S. inflation during the window reduced Fed-hike pricing and therefore improved JPY asymmetry at the margin.

### Where consensus could be wrong

**Consensus too bullish JPY:**
If the BOJ delivers only gradual tightening, Japan’s imported-energy burden stays high and U.S. yields remain elevated, the carry trade can keep USD/JPY around 160 or push it back toward the 165 tactical tail despite intervention risk. A failure to follow through on the newly priced September hike would be particularly damaging because spot was already weak despite expectations of tighter policy.

**Consensus too cautious JPY:**
If the September BOJ hike is followed by explicit guidance toward a quarterly cadence while softer U.S. inflation closes the Fed-hike window, the rate differential could narrow faster than the Reuters/MUFG/ING forecast paths assumed. Renewed coordinated intervention near 160 would amplify such a fundamental turn rather than merely provide temporary support.

**Key falsification:**
The constructive medium-term JPY view requires **actual policy-differential compression**. BOJ delivery/faster guidance combined with fading Fed-hike pricing should move USD/JPY sustainably below the 156–158 constructive cluster; continued trading near or above 160 despite that combination would indicate that energy, fiscal and structural carry forces are materially stronger than the consensus framework assumes.

The window began with USD/JPY near the 163–164 area and institutional commentary still focused on the inability of gradual BoJ tightening to overcome the US–Japan yield differential, high energy costs and strong-dollar backdrop. ### Bottom line

**Pair convention: USD/JPY — a lower pair means a stronger yen.** The dominant public institutional view during 29 June–24 July remained **medium-term constructive JPY / bearish USD/JPY**, but conviction was increasingly tactical rather than clean. On 1 July, the Reuters FX poll still showed median forecasts for USD/JPY around **159 at end-Q3, 156 at year-end and 154 in 12 months**, even though spot had already weakened to around 163. The thesis was gradual yen recovery through further BoJ normalization and, eventually, less supportive US rate differentials rather than an imminent spot reversal.

The bank sample was consistent with that direction but not aggressive in the near term. MUFG’s 1 July profile had **160 for Q3 and 158 for Q4**, BNP Paribas Wealth Management had **158 at 3M and 155 at 12M**, while BNP Paribas Economic Research’s mid-June Q4 forecast was **160**. ING likewise retained a **158 year-end** target, but explicitly allowed USD/JPY to trade around **164–165 before intervention**.

The key change through the window was therefore **spot convergence away from, not forecast convergence toward, the constructive-yen cluster**. USD/JPY touched 161.97 on 29 June, traded around 162–163 through early July, reached 163.23 on 21 July and was around 163.84 on 24 July. By the close, the medium-term recovery consensus had not disappeared, but the near-term asymmetry had deteriorated: strong US rates/dollar momentum, energy-import sensitivity, still-wide carry and doubts about how fast the BoJ could tighten outweighed valuation.

**Public-bank Q4/year-end range:** USD/JPY **158–160** (MUFG 158; ING 158; BNP Paribas Economic Research 160).

**Constructive JPY cluster:** USD/JPY **155–158** across the visible 3M–12M bank targets, with the Reuters year-end survey median at **156**.

**More cautious / tactical view:** USD/JPY **164–165** was a credible near-term overshoot zone in ING’s 30 June analysis; RSM’s Joe Brusuelas went further on 17 July, arguing the pair could move **through 170** before a durable reversal, while putting fair value around **157–158**.

**Bias at window close:** **medium-term JPY constructive, near-term tactically bearish / poor asymmetry** — spot near 164 was materially weaker than the 155–160 public forecast cluster and still moving against it.

### What changed through the data window

At the start of the window, USD/JPY was already at a four-decade extreme near 162 despite the June BoJ hike to 1.00%, with Reuters attributing the pressure to a hawkish Fed, wide rate differentials and broad dollar strength. The 1 July Reuters poll nonetheless showed strategists retaining a gradual yen-recovery profile rather than capitulating to the spot move.

Through early and mid-July, **forecasts were comparatively sticky while the tactical path deteriorated**. MUFG and BNP retained medium-term appreciation targets, but ING explicitly framed 164–165 as plausible before intervention and stressed that intervention could slow rather than reverse a fundamentally driven USD/JPY rise. By 17 July, the visible cautious tail had widened further, with RSM arguing that 170+ was possible before policy and growth fundamentals could reassert themselves.

Late in the window, sourced BoJ reporting introduced a somewhat more hawkish policy-path signal, and markets were pricing roughly 25bp of additional BoJ tightening for the year, but this did not trigger durable yen strength: USD/JPY remained around 163 on 22 July and near 164 on 24 July. The market therefore closed the window with **a medium-term valuation/normalization case for stronger JPY, but a near-term price signal still dominated by USD rates, energy and intervention uncertainty**.

### Main consensus drivers

**US–Japan rate differential / carry:** The June BoJ hike narrowed the gap only marginally, while Fed hawkishness and elevated US yields preserved strong USD carry support. The constructive-yen forecasts required further BoJ normalization and/or a later easing of US rate pressure.

**BoJ normalization / policy credibility:** Public forecasts assumed additional tightening, but the key debate was cadence. Reuters’ July economist poll showed a broad expectation of another 25bp hike by December, while the 22 July market tape raised the possibility of a faster path without forcing a July move.

**Energy / external terms of trade:** Renewed oil-price pressure was especially adverse for Japan because it worsened the import bill and imported inflation, limiting the yen’s ability to behave as a clean defensive currency during the conflict shock.

**Intervention / policy coordination:** Intervention risk capped the clean USD/JPY upside, but repeated warnings and earlier operations had not produced a durable trend reversal. The market increasingly treated intervention as a volatility and level constraint rather than a substitute for a change in the underlying rate differential.

### Where consensus could be wrong

**Consensus too bullish JPY:** If US inflation/rates stay high, the Fed remains hawkish, energy prices remain elevated and the BoJ normalizes only slowly, USD/JPY can remain above the public 155–160 target cluster or overshoot further despite cheap valuation and intervention risk.

**Consensus too cautious JPY:** If the 22 July faster-cadence signal is validated, imported inflation forces an earlier BoJ hike and US rate pressure fades, the rate-differential shift could finally align with extreme valuation and intervention risk, producing a faster move toward or through the 155–158 constructive cluster.

**Key falsification:** A durable move in USD/JPY requires **the rates regime to turn with the policy narrative**: faster BoJ tightening and/or materially softer US rate expectations would validate the constructive-yen consensus; continued high US yields plus slow BoJ normalization would falsify it even if intervention temporarily strengthens JPY.

SIGNAL TENSION CHECK

Main contradiction: Services surveys and flash PMIs pointed to resilient activity, while machinery orders, tertiary activity, the trade balance and Tokyo CPI argued for a more cautious hard-data and inflation picture. The survey-versus-hard-data divergence was the central analytical tension of the window.

Why it matters: The BoJ reaction function is anchored to hard data, wages and sustained inflation confirmation, not survey optimism alone. If capex and external weakness persisted into Q1, the later-2026 next-hike timeline became the default; if PMI resilience translated into hard activity and wage confirmation, the timeline could compress.

Implication: The BoJ remained on hold through at least Q2 2026 unless wages, core-core inflation or yen/import-price pressure forced a reassessment. The spring wage negotiations were the next regime-defining catalyst.
