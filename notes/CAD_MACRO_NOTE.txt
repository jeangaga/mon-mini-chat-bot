# CAD Macro Note — Canada: Q2 Resilience, Softer Q3 Handoff, BoC Hawkish Pivot Meets Weak Wage Transmission

**As of date:** 9 September 2026
**Horizon:** 4-week impulse window → 3–6 month Canada / CAD / BoC scenario update

## 4-WEEK MACRO SIGNAL BOARD

**Growth:** ~ Strong Q2 reset / softer Q3 handoff
**Labor:** ↓ Headcount recovery stalled; wage pressure clearly cooling
**Inflation:** ~ Headline persistence risk / preferred core still near target
**Financial cond:** ~ External funding strong; relative-rate channel increasingly important
**Policy bias:** Hold with explicit hawkish optionality; hike hurdle remains data-dependent
**Political risk:** ↑ Canada-US trade conflict is the main macro-policy risk

## Executive Summary

Canada has exited the recession-risk regime, but has not entered a durable reacceleration. Q2 GDP at 3.3% annualized, the positive Q1 revision, stronger consumption and investment and continued manufacturing expansion establish a materially stronger starting point than assumed earlier in the year. The subsequent handoff is weaker: July GDP was approximately flat, the exceptional trade cushion normalized, August employment fell and near-term business indicators softened. The correct regime is therefore **stronger starting level / weaker marginal momentum**, not renewed recession and not sustained above-trend growth.

The most important policy change is the **2 September BoC reaction-function pivot**. The Bank held at 2.25%, but removed its prior comfort language and explicitly opened the possibility of one or more hikes if inflation remains too high. Stronger realized activity allowed the Bank to raise its response coefficient to inflation persistence. That communication initially generated a much larger market reaction than the underlying macro data: swaps briefly priced roughly even odds of an October hike and close to a full hike by December, while most economists continued to expect no 2026 move.

The 4 September labour report immediately raised the execution hurdle. Employment fell 41.7k, participation weakened and wage growth slowed to 2.0%. The headcount data are noisy—public-sector attrition and temporary employment effects matter—but the wage signal is harder to dismiss. The wage-price transmission channel is currently weakening rather than strengthening. Preferred core inflation remains around target, ex-energy producer inflation is soft and shelter disinflation continues. The binding issue is therefore **prospective energy/tariff pass-through into broader inflation**, not an already-established domestic inflation spiral.

This leaves the BoC in a genuine hawkish hold rather than a confirmed hiking cycle. The majority institutional view remains hold through year-end; the hawkish minority sees September as the start of a tightening sequence in communication terms and expects inflation persistence to force implementation. The base case remains 2.25%, but the asymmetry has changed: cuts are no longer live without a broader growth break, while hikes require considerably more realized breadth than the headline CPI alone.

For CAD, the medium-term macro case remains constructive but the tactical setup is more conditional. USD/CAD has already moved materially away from the July highs, while published bank targets remain generally stronger-CAD than spot. Relative BoC/Fed pricing, not oil alone, is increasingly the key FX channel. Continued trade damage plus a wider US-Canada front-end differential would re-open CAD downside; trade stabilization and sustained Canadian growth would make the 1.36–1.37 area more credible.

## Growth / Activity

Canadian growth has undergone a genuine **level reset**, but the momentum signal is already softer.

The Q2 national accounts removed the recession narrative more decisively than the 3.3% headline alone implies. Q1 contraction was revised away, household consumption accelerated, business fixed investment strengthened, residential investment improved and final domestic demand was firm. Inventories subtracted materially, improving the quality of the final-demand signal. The recovery was also broader than the earlier energy-heavy monthly data suggested.

The correct implication is not that Canada has moved into a sustained above-potential regime. It is that the economy entered the renewed trade shock with substantially more buffer than previously assumed.

The post-Q2 handoff is weaker. July industry GDP was approximately flat. The merchandise trade surplus collapsed from exceptional Q2 levels as energy and precious-metal support normalized. Housing starts remain one of the clearer weak pockets. August CFIB short-term expectations and staffing intentions weakened. Manufacturing remains expansionary, with domestic orders and output holding up, but foreign orders remain under pressure and much of the August survey preceded the deterioration in Canada-US trade relations.

There is a genuine debate over whether these are three manifestations of one slowdown or largely separate normalizations after unusually strong Q2 data. The more cautious interpretation links flat July GDP, trade normalization and softer employment into a common loss of momentum. The alternative view treats July/August as a predictable payback after strong activity, trade and hiring prints.

The distinction matters for the 3–6 month scenario. For now, the evidence supports:

**Q2 resilience confirmed → Q3 momentum softer → outright contraction unconfirmed.**

The growth floor is being carried by consumption, investment and still-resilient manufacturing activity. Housing and US-facing external demand remain the clearest weak pockets.

## Labor / Employment

Labour has moved from apparent recovery into **mixed softening**, but the headline employment sequence is more distorted than the wage signal.

July had appeared to confirm the spring rebound: employment rose 75.1k, unemployment fell to 6.4% and participation improved. August then reversed part of that move with employment down 41.7k, lower participation and a falling employment rate.

The unchanged 6.4% unemployment rate should not be treated as clean evidence of stability. Labour-force participation declined, Canadian population growth has slowed materially and lower labour-supply growth can mechanically make unemployment look better even when labour demand is weak. The level of unemployment therefore carries less information about economic slack than it did under rapid population growth.

The monthly headcount data themselves are noisy. July contained temporary census/World Cup-related support, while August included planned public-sector attrition. Manufacturing employment still rose by around 22k, supporting the PMI's stronger sectoral employment signal. This argues against extrapolating one negative headline directly into recession.

The more important policy signal is wages:

**June ~3.3% → July 2.8% → August 2.0%.**

That trajectory materially weakens the domestic second-round inflation case. Firms may still pass through energy, tariffs and imported costs, but there is currently little evidence that labour compensation is reinforcing the shock.

Labour therefore no longer supports the September hiking narrative in the way July did. It also does not yet support cuts. The state is:

**hiring/headcount softer + labour supply softer + wage pressure clearly softer + sectoral activity still resilient.**

A second weak LFS accompanied by falling hours and rising unemployment would materially change the regime. One noisy negative employment print does not.

## Inflation

Canada remains in a **headline inflation / underlying inflation divergence**.

July CPI rose to 3.0% y/y and gave the BoC a clear reason to increase vigilance. Gasoline, refined petroleum pressure and temporary travel effects accounted for much of the move, while short-run core momentum firmed enough to prevent a clean look-through call.

But the level of underlying inflation remains much calmer:

**Median:** around 2.0%
**Trim:** around 1.9%
**Ex-gasoline CPI:** around 2.2%
**Shelter:** continuing to decelerate
**Wages:** 2.0% y/y in August
**Ex-energy producer prices:** soft

The producer-price pipeline reinforces the distinction. Refined petroleum prices rose sharply while raw-material and ex-energy factory prices weakened. That is consistent with a downstream energy/refining shock, not generalized producer-price acceleration.

The binding issue is now whether the external cost shock develops **breadth and persistence**.

The Bank is watching a potential transmission chain:

energy / tariffs / supply disruption
→ business input costs
→ selling prices
→ broader core goods/services
→ expectations and wages.

The first two stages are visible. The final stages are not.

This matters because the hawkish policy tail rests more on a **forecast of propagation** than on realized generalized inflation. The next CPI sequence must therefore do more than keep headline inflation around 3%. It needs to show that median/trim, services breadth or import-sensitive categories are moving persistently higher.

Inflation currently blocks easing more clearly than it compels tightening.

## Last Central Bank Meeting + Current Policy Assumption

The latest Bank of Canada meeting was **2 September 2026**.

The Bank held the overnight rate at **2.25%**, with the Bank Rate at 2.50% and deposit rate at 2.20%.

The unchanged decision concealed a material shift in the conditional policy map.

Stronger Q2 GDP, the removal of the technical-recession narrative, firmer household demand and the July labour rebound reduced the need for the Bank to insure against immediate weakness. At the same time, prolonged energy pressure and worsening trade frictions raised the probability that cost shocks could become persistent.

The Bank removed its previous characterization that policy was at the “right level.” Macklem described inflation around 3% as **“too high for comfort”** and explicitly stated that the Bank was prepared to raise rates, including more than once, if inflation remained too high.

This should be interpreted as a **higher inflation reaction coefficient**, not as proof that the hike trigger has already fired.

The market initially treated the communication much more aggressively than the economist consensus. Immediately after the meeting, swaps moved toward roughly a 50% probability of an October hike and close to a full 25bp hike by December, with additional tightening priced further into 2027. The majority of economists nevertheless continued to forecast no move through year-end. Scotiabank represents the clearest hawkish institutional tail, with a tightening path beginning in Q4; CIBC, TD, BMO and others remain substantially more cautious.

The August LFS subsequently weakened the hiking case, particularly through wages. Exact post-LFS OIS retracement is less cleanly observable, but the direction is clear: the employment report challenged the aggressive immediate post-BoC repricing without eliminating the newly established hawkish policy tail.

### CURRENT POLICY ASSUMPTION

**Base case: BoC holds at 2.25% through the next 3–6 months, with October 28 the key forecast-reset meeting and a live late-2026 hike tail.**

A more hawkish path requires preferred core inflation to move persistently above the low-2s, services/inflation breadth to increase and the post-Q2 growth slowdown to remain shallow. Wage reacceleration would materially strengthen the case.

A more dovish path requires labour weakness to broaden beyond monthly noise into weaker hours, higher unemployment and softer demand while preferred core inflation remains anchored.

The BoC is reacting most strongly to **whether energy/tariff inflation becomes persistent**, while trade damage determines how much freedom it has to respond.

## Rates / FX / Financial Conditions

The defining market tension is now **market pricing versus economist consensus**.

The 2 September BoC communication briefly shifted Canadian rates from a conditional hike tail toward an actual late-2026 tightening base case in market pricing. Roughly half an October hike and almost a full December move were priced immediately after Macklem's press conference, while longer-dated pricing contemplated several hikes through 2027.

That is substantially more hawkish than the modal economist forecast.

The August labour report challenged that pricing through both growth and inflation channels. Falling employment weakens the demand case; 2.0% wages weaken the persistence case. The correct rates configuration is therefore:

**BoC rhetoric supports a hawkish premium; realized data have not yet validated the full tightening path.**

The next major repricing catalyst is CPI breadth. A second-order trigger is whether the Q3 slowdown remains shallow enough for the Bank to act without creating a larger growth break.

The Fed side matters materially for CAD. The US-Canada front-end differential remains an active driver, and the market entered mid-September with a much more hawkish Fed path than earlier in the summer. A Fed hike without BoC follow-through would widen the relative-rate disadvantage and create a near-term USD/CAD upside channel even if Canadian domestic fundamentals remain acceptable.

CAD has already appreciated substantially from the July highs above 1.40–1.42. Published public-bank forecasts remain broadly constructive, with much of the cluster around roughly **1.36–1.37 by Q4**, while a broader forecaster distribution is closer to the high-1.38/1.39 region.

The implication is more nuanced than simple “upside exhausted.”

The medium-term CAD thesis is still alive. The public institutional distribution is generally more CAD-constructive than spot. But realization now requires catalysts:

* narrower relative rates,
* trade stabilization,
* sustained Canadian investment/growth,
* continued export diversification.

The currency is increasingly trading through **relative rates first, trade risk second, oil as a floor rather than a standalone catalyst**.

Financial conditions themselves are not tight enough to imply an endogenous downturn. Record foreign purchases of Canadian government bonds and the Q2 current-account swing demonstrate strong external absorption. But the durability of those capital flows is uncertain—they may reflect relative-yield demand, reserve allocation, hedging or energy-income recycling rather than a permanent structural CAD regime.

## Political / Policy Events

Political/policy risk is macro-relevant and almost entirely external.

The deterioration in Canada-US trade relations accelerated during the latest impulse window. Trade negotiations broke down in late August, new US tariffs materially increased the effective restriction on selected Canadian goods and Canadian counter-tariffs followed in early September.

This shock is not cleanly dovish or hawkish.

**Growth channel:** weaker exports, delayed investment, hiring restraint and lower business confidence.

**Inflation channel:** higher imported costs, disrupted supply chains, counter-tariff pass-through and potential CAD depreciation.

That two-sided structure explains why the BoC can sound more hawkish at the same time that the growth outlook becomes less secure.

The geography of Canadian exports is an important partial cushion. The US share of exports has fallen materially while non-US exports have reached record levels, particularly toward Europe. This is evidence of genuine diversification rather than simple export destruction.

The scale question remains unresolved. The US market is still too large for diversification to neutralize a severe bilateral trade shock quickly. Diversification improves resilience; it does not eliminate the US growth constraint.

Domestic Canadian political developments are not currently an independent regime driver.

## 5 Key Releases / Events

### 1. Bank of Canada Rate Decision — 2 September 2026

The reaction-function pivot. The Bank held at 2.25% but removed its previous comfort language and explicitly introduced multiple-hike conditionality. Market pricing briefly moved much further hawkish than the economist consensus. This event defines the current policy asymmetry.

### 2. Labour Force Survey — August — 4 September 2026

The immediate challenge to the BoC pivot. Employment fell 41.7k, participation weakened and wage growth slowed to 2.0%. Monthly employment is compositionally noisy; wage deceleration is the cleaner reason to raise the hike hurdle.

### 3. GDP, Income and Expenditure — Q2 — 28 August 2026

The regime-reset release. GDP grew 3.3% annualized and Q1 contraction was revised away. Domestic demand and investment were stronger than the earlier energy-only interpretation implied. The release eliminated recession as the central scenario but did not prove persistence into Q3.

### 4. CPI — July — 17 August 2026

The inflation anchor for the September communication. Headline CPI reached 3.0%, while median/trim remained around target. The release moved Canada from clean disinflation toward a persistence watch without establishing generalized inflation.

### 5. Canada-US Trade Escalation — late August / early September 2026

The binding policy shock. Trade negotiations deteriorated, US tariffs increased and Canadian countermeasures followed. The shock simultaneously weakens demand and raises cost-push risk, creating the stagflationary constraint that makes the BoC hold unusually unstable.

## ARC

### Data window: January to April 2026

Canada entered 2026 with weak domestic momentum, housing sensitivity, soft business conditions and credible recession risk. Inflation breadth was improving and labour deterioration kept easing optionality alive, but trade uncertainty prevented a clean benign-disinflation scenario. The regime was **weak growth / contained underlying inflation / two-sided BoC risk**.

### Data window: May to June 2026

The energy shock complicated the picture. Higher energy prices lifted headline CPI, producer prices and household fuel costs but simultaneously improved Canada's terms of trade, trade balance and manufacturing revenues. Labour rebounded and activity stopped deteriorating. Preferred core remained near target, creating a BoC stalemate: insufficient growth weakness for cuts, insufficient inflation breadth for hikes.

### Data window: July to mid-August 2026

The domestic rebound became more credible. Employment strengthened, consumption improved, manufacturing stayed expansionary and monthly GDP confirmed a genuine Q2 recovery. June CPI delivered a strong underlying-disinflation print before July headline inflation moved back to 3%. Trade and geopolitical risks simultaneously worsened. The regime moved toward **better growth / near-target core / larger forward cost-push tail**.

### Data window: late August to 9 September 2026

Q2 GDP formally removed the recession narrative and established a stronger starting point. The BoC used that resilience to raise its willingness to defend against persistent inflation on 2 September, triggering an aggressive market repricing toward late-2026 hikes. The subsequent trade and labour releases immediately challenged implementation: the external windfall faded, employment declined and wages slowed to 2.0%. The current state is **stronger level / softer momentum / explicit BoC hawkish optionality / persistence not yet proven**.

## Trigger Map

### Reopen easing debate / dovish reweighting

A second stage of labour deterioration—rising unemployment, weaker hours and softer Q3 activity—combined with median/trim staying around 2% would convert the current slowdown from normalization into demand damage. **Policy:** hike tail collapses; cuts re-enter the 2027 distribution. **Market:** Canadian front end rallies and CAD loses relative-rate support.

### Hold-longer / credibility constraint hardens

Median/trim and services inflation move persistently above the low-2s while growth remains positive and labour stabilizes. **Macro:** energy/tariff pressure has become broad enough to matter. **Policy:** September hawkish rhetoric becomes executable; October/December hike pricing rebuilds.

### Trade / relative-rate risk premium widens

Canada-US trade damage deepens while the Fed remains more hawkish than the BoC. **Macro:** weaker Canadian growth coincides with an adverse rate differential and potential FX pass-through. **Market:** USD/CAD upside reopens even without a Canadian recession; BoC faces a more acute stagflationary constraint.

### Constructive stabilization / CAD quality improves

Q3 growth stays positive, labour stabilizes without wage reacceleration, core inflation remains near target and trade tensions stop worsening. **Macro:** Q2 resilience proves durable enough without overheating. **Policy/market:** hold extends, excess hike pricing fades and CAD appreciation becomes a growth/trade-quality story rather than a tightening story.

## Debate / Public Tape Tension

The dominant public narrative broadly confirms a **stronger starting point / softer handoff / BoC hold with a genuine hike option** regime. The recession story has been discarded, but no major institutional cluster is treating Q2's 3.3% growth as evidence of sustained above-potential expansion. The more important divergence is between rates pricing and economist forecasts: the September 2 communication briefly pushed swaps toward roughly even odds of an October hike and close to a full move by December, while the modal bank/economist view remained no 2026 change. The subsequent labour report weakened the market's most aggressive interpretation but did not erase the policy premium. For FX, institutional forecasts remain generally more CAD-constructive than spot, but the transmission has shifted toward relative BoC/Fed rates and trade risk rather than a mechanical oil beta.

The real institutional disagreement is **hold-through-2026 versus a late-2026 tightening sequence**, not cuts versus hikes at the next meeting. The hawkish camp interprets Macklem's removal of “right level” language and explicit multiple-hike conditionality as the beginning of a genuine tightening regime; Scotiabank is the clearest version of that view. The larger hold camp argues that preferred core inflation around 2%, August wage growth at 2.0%, softer employment and unresolved trade damage leave the data well short of the threshold required to execute that guidance. Labour deserves a particularly careful read: July and August headcount prints contain temporary/public-sector distortions, while slower labour-supply growth makes the 6.4% unemployment rate look stronger than the underlying slack picture may be. The cleanest signal is therefore wage disinflation rather than either monthly employment headline. On CAD, the market debate is similarly two-sided: published bank targets still point toward stronger CAD, but a hawkish Fed / patient BoC differential can dominate that medium-term convergence tactically.

The scenario breaks on a small number of near-term gates. Persistent movement in median/trim and services inflation above the low-2s would validate the BoC's persistence thesis and pull October/December tightening back toward the centre of the distribution. A second weak labour report with falling hours or a broader Q3 activity contraction would instead turn the current handoff from normalization into a genuine growth break and remove most of the hike premium. Trade is the third gate: de-escalation would strengthen growth, reduce the stagflationary constraint and improve CAD carry quality; further escalation would simultaneously weaken activity and raise imported-cost risk. Relative US policy remains the fourth transmission channel—continued Fed hawkishness without BoC follow-through can weaken CAD even if the domestic Canadian macro regime remains fundamentally stable.

## Bottom Line

Canada has exited recession risk but has not entered a clean reacceleration. Q2 established much stronger underlying resilience; the July-August handoff shows softer momentum without yet proving contraction. The BoC has genuinely raised its inflation reaction coefficient, but preferred core near target and wages at 2.0% mean the data have not validated a hiking cycle. Market pricing can remain more hawkish than economist consensus, but further tightening requires realized inflation breadth rather than headline persistence alone. CAD retains a medium-term constructive distribution, while the near-term path is increasingly controlled by trade risk and the BoC/Fed differential.

**Base case: BoC holds at 2.25%; Q3 slows but avoids a break; underlying inflation stays near target; the late-2026 hike tail remains live but untriggered.**

CAD Macro Note — Canada: BoC Stalemate, Labour Rebound, Headline Inflation Constraint, CAD FX Drag

As of date: 30 June 2026
Data anchor: macro releases through 22 June; public-tape overlay through late June
Horizon: 4-week impulse window → 3–6 month Canada / CAD / BoC scenario update

4-WEEK MACRO SIGNAL BOARD

Growth: ~ Weak Q1, Q2 rebound possible but quality uneven
Labor: ↑ May rebound removes labour-break cut case; wages cooler
Inflation: ↑ Headline above band, trim / median still contained
Financial cond: ↓ CAD weaker; Canada–US spread / USD / gold drag dominate
Policy bias: Hold; scenario-symmetric, tolerance mildly hawkish
Political risk: ↑ CUSMA / US trade and energy path are binding gates

Executive Summary

Canada is no longer best framed as simply weak-growth / oil-cushioned / contained-core. The cleaner regime is BoC stalemate: growth is not strong enough to validate a broad tightening cycle, but inflation and labour are not weak enough to reopen a near-term easing path. The 10 June BoC hold at 2.25% formalized that bind: weak activity and trade uncertainty argue against hikes, while headline inflation above target and energy pass-through risk block cuts.

The biggest correction is labour. The April softening was reversed by the 5 June May LFS: employment rose strongly, unemployment fell to 6.6%, and full-time jobs surged. Wage growth cooled, so this is not a wage-inflation shock, but it materially reduces the cut-tail narrative. The labour gate now points to stabilization / slack moderation rather than imminent demand break.

Inflation is the binding constraint. May CPI rose to 3.2% y/y, above the BoC target band, while median and trim stayed at 2.1% / 2.0%. That preserves the look-through story, but only conditionally. Food inflation, ex-gasoline firmness, IPPI pressure, CAD depreciation and 3-month annualized trim / median momentum leave the BoC with less tolerance for further breadth.

CAD is the other major reframing. Canada still benefits from energy in nominal trade and manufacturing channels, but CAD spot is not currently trading as an oil-cushioned currency. The marginal FX tape is broad USD strength, adverse Canada–US 2-year spreads, CAD weakness near 1.42, and a stronger gold / bullion channel than the simple WTI channel. The base case is BoC hold, but the market prices a more live December hike than a simple residual premium.

Growth / Activity

Growth is weak but not cleanly recessionary. Q1 GDP was flat / slightly negative on the annualized convention, Q4 was revised lower, March GDP contracted, and final domestic demand softened. That justifies keeping Canada below the stronger DM-growth bucket.

The weak-growth label needs qualification. Q1 was distorted by trade and gold-import mechanics, and several desks treat the recession label as premature. April trade improved through an energy-linked surplus, manufacturing sales rose 4.2% to a record level, real manufacturing sales rose 1.8%, and early Q2 activity can rebound if mining, energy and manufacturing normalize. The growth story is therefore not “recession confirmed”; it is “Q1 weak, Q2 rebound test pending.”

The domestic-demand quality remains poor. Building permits fell, wholesale volumes declined, CFIB stayed sub-50, the 3-month CFIB index deteriorated, retail volumes were flat, and core ex-fuel / ex-autos retail fell for a second month. Energy lifts nominal income and producer revenue, but households and small businesses are not showing a broad reacceleration.

Labor / Employment

Labour has moved from softening to rebound / normalization. The May LFS is now the decisive labour anchor: employment rose 88k, unemployment fell to 6.6%, and full-time employment surged. This reverses much of the early-2026 labour softness and makes a labour-break cut case much harder to sustain.

The wage detail keeps the signal from turning hawkish outright. Permanent-employee wage growth cooled sharply to 3.2% from 4.8%. That reduces wage-price-spiral risk and supports the BoC’s ability to look through first-round energy inflation, but the employment rebound removes the strongest domestic argument for early cuts.

The labour gate is now asymmetric. A renewed unemployment move above 7% with weaker hours and softer wages would reopen easing. But absent that, labour is no longer the reason to cut; it is a stabilization input that allows the BoC to wait while inflation breadth is tested.

Inflation

Inflation is a headline breach with contained underlying measures. May CPI rose 1.0% m/m and 3.2% y/y, above the 3.0% poll and above the BoC’s 1–3% target band. Gasoline was the dominant driver, but ex-gasoline CPI firmed to 2.2%, and food inflation remains a visible thorn.

The preferred core measures still support look-through. Median stayed at 2.1%, trim stayed at 2.0%, and CPI ex food and energy was subdued. The core interior does not yet validate generalized inflation or a full hiking regime.

The risk is momentum and breadth. Three-month annualized trim / median at 2.3%, food pressure, IPPI still elevated in annual terms, and CAD depreciation all matter because the BoC’s trigger is not gasoline itself; it is persistence. If June CPI confirms gasoline reversal and trim / median remain anchored, the plateau extends. If core breadth rises, the December-hike pricing becomes harder to fade.

Last Central Bank Meeting + Current Policy Assumption

The latest Bank of Canada policy meeting was 10 June 2026. The BoC held the overnight rate at 2.25%, with the Bank Rate at 2.50% and deposit rate at 2.20%. It was the fifth consecutive hold. The next meeting is 15 July 2026, with an MPR.

The meeting defined a two-directional hold, but the tolerance is not perfectly symmetric. The BoC can cut if US trade restrictions and weak activity dominate, but the inflation language is more forceful on the hawkish side: it will not let higher energy prices become persistent inflation, and consecutive increases remain possible if breadth develops. The framework is scenario-symmetric, but mildly hawkish in credibility tolerance.

CURRENT POLICY ASSUMPTION

Base case for the next 3–6 months: BoC holds at 2.25%, including the July MPR, unless one of the two tails is activated. A dovish reweighting requires renewed labour weakness, Q2 growth disappointment, tariff / CUSMA deterioration and core inflation staying near target. A hawkish reweighting requires headline inflation persistence plus pass-through into trim, median, services, wages, expectations or FX import prices. The BoC is waiting for confirmation of either growth damage or inflation breadth; the current data support patience, not action.

Rates / FX / Financial Conditions

Canada front-end pricing is more hawkish than a pure economist hold consensus. July hold is the base case, but OIS carries a meaningful year-end hike profile, with December hike risk more than a residual premium. The economist counter remains strong: RBC / BMO / CIBC-style hold-through-2026 arguments rest on contained core, soft real demand and the likelihood that gasoline reverses.

CAD has weakened more than the macro-only framing would imply. The currency is near 1.42 against USD, and the current marginal drivers are broad USD strength, unfavourable Canada–US 2-year spreads, and bullion / gold dynamics rather than WTI alone. The oil channel still matters for nominal trade and CPI, but it is not currently delivering clean CAD spot support.

Financial conditions are therefore tighter through FX and external pricing, not through domestic credit stress. CAD weakness can become an inflation input if it feeds import prices, while the US spread keeps USD/CAD supported unless US data soften or Canadian yields reprice higher. CAD upside now needs either a trade-policy relief catalyst, firmer Canadian rates, or a reversal in the gold / USD drag.

Political / Policy Events

Political / policy risk is regime-relevant through US trade and CUSMA, not domestic Canadian politics. The BoC’s downside scenario is explicitly tied to trade restrictions because the transmission runs through exports, investment, business confidence and labour.

CUSMA uncertainty is moving from background risk to live macro gate. An unfavourable or prolonged negotiation path would deepen hardship in trade-exposed sectors and could spread to broader demand. That would activate the BoC’s dovish tail if core inflation remains contained.

Energy geopolitics remains the opposite gate. Oil persistence supports nominal activity and lifts headline CPI, but if June gasoline declines are sustained, the headline-inflation tail fades. Oil normalization would not automatically create cuts; it would remove the inflation emergency and extend the plateau unless domestic demand weakens again.

CAD Macro Note — Canada: Hold-With-Look-Through, Two-Way Optionality, Tariff/Oil Gates Binding

As of date: 27 May 2026
Horizon: 4-week impulse window → 3–6 month Canada / CAD / BoC scenario update

4-WEEK MACRO SIGNAL BOARD

Growth: ~ Mixed; Q1 floor, Q2 quality test
Labor: ↓ Softening, but not breaking
Inflation: ~ Headline energy shock, core cooling, IPPI pass-through risk
Financial cond: ~ Restrictive through real demand / housing sensitivity, not disorderly
Policy bias: Hold / plateau with symmetric optionality
Political risk: ↑ Trade / tariff and energy channels are binding macro gates

Executive Summary

Canada is best framed as a hold-with-look-through regime, not a clean easing setup. The latest 4-week impulse confirms the BoC can look through the first-round energy shock: April headline CPI rose to 2.8% y/y, but the preferred core mix softened, labour deteriorated, real retail volumes fell and small-business confidence weakened. That combination lowers the probability that gasoline immediately becomes broad domestic inflation. Statistics Canada confirms April CPI at 2.8% y/y and the May CPI release is not due until 22 June, so the disputed 1.7% headline claim should be discarded.

The public-policy and market distribution is more two-sided than a simple dovish read. The dominant institutional base case is that the BoC holds at 2.25% through the near term, while the market has been pricing a residual H2 hike premium rather than an outright cut path. The labour and CPI data clipped some hike expectations, but did not flip the front end into a pure easing regime. Reuters reported that after the April jobs miss investors were still pricing 38bp of BoC tightening by December, down from 44bp before the release.

The binding constraint is now two external gates plus one domestic validation gate: US tariffs / trade uncertainty, oil persistence, and whether IPPI / energy pass-through enters trim, median, wages or services. The BoC’s April framework is explicitly conditional on moderate growth under tariffs and inflation easing back after the energy shock, not on a one-way cut cycle. The base case is a plateau with look-through; the tails are cuts if trade / labour deteriorates materially, and hikes if oil persistence generalizes into core.

Growth / Activity

Growth has a floor, but the floor is backward-looking and compositionally imperfect. February GDP rose 0.2% m/m, manufacturing PMI improved, trade swung into surplus, Ivey rebounded, wholesale trade beat, housing starts surprised higher and building permits recovered. That prevents a recessionary read.

The quality issue is more important than the headline sequence. A large share of the recent nominal resilience is commodity-linked, price-flattered, volatile or front-loaded. Trade strength was energy / gold heavy, manufacturing sales were materially supported by petroleum and coal, retail sales looked strong in dollars but weak in real volumes, and permits were non-residential-led. Q1 strength can therefore coexist with a weaker Q2 impulse if tariff front-running fades and the fuel-cost squeeze persists.

The cleanest near-term growth drag is not hard GDP yet; it is real demand quality. CFIB confidence collapsed to 46.3, retail volumes fell, and labour softened. This argues for caution on extrapolating Q1 stabilization into a durable domestic reacceleration. Canada is not breaking, but the latest impulse moves from “resilient” to Q1 floor / Q2 quality test.

Labor / Employment

Labour has softened enough to cap the BoC’s hawkish impulse, but not enough to force a cut. April employment was little changed at -18k, the unemployment rate rose to 6.9%, full-time employment fell by 47k, and full-time jobs were down 111k over the first four months of 2026. Wage growth slowed to 4.5% y/y from 4.7% in March.

The important nuance is that the labour market is softening, not collapsing. The unemployment rate has risen 0.4pp since January, but remains below the 7.1% peaks of August–September 2025; the layoff rate was still in line with pre-pandemic norms. That makes the signal dovish for the reaction function through slack and weaker hiring, but not recessionary enough to make cuts the dominant base case.

For the BoC, labour functions as a pass-through buffer. Weak full-time employment and rising unemployment reduce the risk that energy inflation becomes a wage-price spiral, but wages at 4.5% y/y still prevent full comfort. The labour gate becomes decisive if unemployment moves through 7% with wage moderation and weak hours.

Inflation

Inflation is now a headline-energy / core-look-through problem, not a generalized domestic inflation shock. April CPI rose to 2.8% y/y, below the 3.1% poll embedded in the CAD week file, while BoC core, median and trim moved around 2.0–2.1%. The correct policy signal is not that inflation collapsed; it is that the energy shock has not yet generalized.

The producer side keeps the regime two-sided. IPPI rose strongly in April after a hotter March revision, and the producer-consumer wedge is now the main inflation risk. This does not override the April core relief, because the BoC’s baseline already assumes limited pass-through. It does mean that hot IPPI cannot be ignored if it begins to appear in core goods, services, wages or expectations.

The hawkish tail requires visible confirmation in trim / median, not just hot gasoline or factory-gate prices. If May/June CPI shows trim and median reaccelerating above the low-2s, the “look-through” assumption weakens quickly. If trim and median stay contained, the BoC can continue to treat the energy shock as a relative-price squeeze hitting real demand rather than a reason to tighten.

Last Central Bank Meeting + Current Policy Assumption

The latest BoC policy meeting was 29 April 2026. The Bank held the overnight rate at 2.25%, with the Bank Rate at 2.50% and deposit rate at 2.20%. The next scheduled rate announcement is 10 June 2026.

The meeting was a conditional hold. The Bank’s April framework expected moderate growth as the economy adjusts to US tariffs, higher inflation from oil, and inflation easing back toward 2% in 2027. The 13 May deliberations reinforced that the outlook is conditional on oil normalization and the tariff assumption, with tariff escalation on the dovish-growth side and persistent oil pass-through on the hawkish-inflation side.

CURRENT POLICY ASSUMPTION

Base case for the next 3–6 months: BoC holds at 2.25% through the near term, with symmetric optionality rather than a one-way easing bias. A dovish reweighting requires tariff escalation, unemployment moving above 7%, retail-volume weakness persisting and trim / median staying near 2%. A hawkish reweighting requires sustained oil persistence and clear pass-through into trim, median, wages or inflation expectations. The Bank is reacting most to the interaction between trade damage, oil persistence and core pass-through; not to headline gasoline alone.

Rates / FX / Financial Conditions

The front end is better described as hike-risk premium versus economist hold consensus, not cut pricing. Labour and core CPI data reduced the amount of tightening priced, but did not remove it. The rates market is therefore trading the BoC’s hawkish tail as a risk premium, while the modal economics view remains a plateau.

CAD remains range-bound and conditional. The support channels are oil / terms of trade, residual H2 hike pricing, and the absence of a clean domestic recession. The drag channels are softer labour, weak real consumption, tariff uncertainty and the risk that Q1 strength was front-loaded or nominal. The result is not a directional CAD call; it is a macro-relative balance between commodity support and domestic-quality deterioration.

Financial conditions are restrictive enough to slow real demand but not tight enough to force the BoC into immediate relief. Housing starts and permits have improved, but the construction signal is uneven. Retail volumes and CFIB confidence show the fuel shock tightening household and small-business conditions in real terms.

Political / Policy Events

Political / policy risk is regime-relevant through US tariffs / trade policy and energy geopolitics, not domestic Canadian politics. The BoC’s outlook is explicitly conditional on US tariffs remaining stable and oil prices declining over time. That makes trade policy a central reaction-function input, not a background risk.

Tariff escalation would transmit through exports, investment, employment and confidence. It would likely turn the BoC more dovish even if headline inflation stayed elevated, because the shock would be growth-destructive. Energy persistence works in the opposite direction: it tightens real purchasing power and can create an inflation credibility problem if it reaches core.

Current political risk bias is therefore higher than normal. The dominant macro channel is external policy uncertainty, with tariffs on the growth side and oil on the inflation side. CAD risk premium should rise if either channel becomes persistent rather than transitory.
